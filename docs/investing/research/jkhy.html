Business model

Jack Henry & Associates sells the technology backbone that community banks and credit unions run on: core processing platforms that handle deposit, loan, and general-ledger transactions and hold the system of record for account holders, sold across four reporting segments. Core ($768.5M of FY2026 revenue, up 4.8%) is the foundational processing platform itself; Payments ($936.0M, up 7.2%, now the largest segment) covers ATM/debit/credit card processing, bill pay, ACH origination, and remote deposit capture; Complementary ($752.2M, up 8.3%) layers on digital banking, risk management, and other add-on software and hosted services; and Corporate and Other ($87.7M, up 18.3%) is mostly hardware and miscellaneous services. Jack Henry serves more than 7,200 banks and credit unions in aggregate across these lines. The business model's defining trait is switching-cost-driven durability: once a bank's core deposit and loan ledger runs on Jack Henry's platform, ripping it out is a multi-year, operationally risky undertaking, which is why the company has historically retained the overwhelming majority of its processing clients each year and can price multi-year hosting and processing contracts as recurring, subscription-like revenue rather than one-off software sales.

The defining challenge for this DCF — a discounted multiple, a capital-allocation pivot, and thin near-term margin gains

Unlike most richly-valued "quality compounder" names elsewhere in this series, Jack Henry's problem right now is not an expensive multiple — it's the opposite. The stock trades at roughly 24x trailing earnings, about 30% below its own 10-year average P/E near 34x, even as FY2026 closed out as a record year: 7.1% GAAP revenue growth, operating margin up 92 basis points to 25.0%, GAAP EPS up 11.9% to $6.98, record 58 competitive core wins including the company's largest-ever client win (Woodforest National Bank), and free cash flow up sharply. The catch is what comes next: FY2027 guidance calls for non-GAAP revenue growth of just 6.3%–7.3% — essentially a continuation of, not an acceleration from, FY2026's pace — paired with only 20–40 basis points of further operating-margin expansion, as the company absorbs higher cybersecurity, cloud-infrastructure, and AI-related spending alongside its multi-year EC2030 data-center-consolidation project. On top of that, Jack Henry made an unusually aggressive capital-allocation choice in FY2026: it spent $448M on buybacks (122% of free cash flow) at an average price of $152/share, retiring roughly 4% of shares outstanding, and funded part of that by draining its cash balance from $102.0M to just $12.1M and drawing $40M on its credit facility for the first time in years — a modest but real shift away from the historically conservative, near-debt-free balance sheet this name is known for. The market's discounted multiple appears to be pricing that combination: real, guided, but decelerating-relative-to-history growth, margin gains that are being reinvested rather than banked, and a company now willing to lever its balance sheet, however lightly, to buy back stock at a price it evidently considers cheap.

The debt picture

As of June 30, 2026, Jack Henry held $12.1M of cash and cash equivalents against $40M drawn on its revolving credit facility — a small net debt position of $27.9M (-$0.39/share), a reversal from $102.0M of cash and zero debt a year earlier. Management has said the facility is being used, alongside cash on hand, to fund the expanded stock-repurchase program (the board added 5 million shares to the authorization in May 2026, bringing the remaining count to 6.4 million shares), not for any acquisition or liquidity need — this is a discretionary, buyback-driven use of the balance sheet rather than distress. Beta is low at 0.55, reflecting Jack Henry's recurring, high-retention revenue base and historically low volatility; applying CAPM (4.7% risk-free rate, 5% equity risk premium) puts the cost of equity — and, since the current debt load is immaterial relative to the ~$12.1B market cap, the WACC — at roughly 7.5%, one of the lowest discount rates used anywhere in this series.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Each scenario anchors on a different FCF base and growth path to bracket how much of FY2026's acceleration continues. The base case uses the TTM (FY2026) FCF run-rate and credits growth modestly above the top of management's FY2027 guidance range before decaying, essentially betting the recent margin-expansion trend resumes once the current cyber/AI/data-center investment cycle passes; the conservative case starts from FY2025's actual FCF — before FY2026's margin jump — and assumes growth roughly in line with the low end of FY2027 guidance, i.e., that the guided deceleration mostly sticks; the bear case starts from the smoothed 5-year average FCF, which is dragged down by FY2023's unusually low, working-capital-affected free cash flow, and assumes low-single-digit growth, treating both FY2026's margin gain and the FY2027 guidance as largely non-repeating. Every scenario nets out the current $27.9M net debt position and divides by 71.05M shares outstanding.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

JKHY's $169.73 price sits almost exactly on top of the conservative case and far above the bear case — a very different shape from most names in this series, where the market price sits above every scenario. That's a specific, and unusually sober, market bet: investors appear to be pricing something close to "FY2027 guidance mostly holds, but don't extrapolate FY2026's margin jump forward," rather than either the optimistic base case (continued margin expansion once the current investment cycle rolls off) or the pessimistic bear case (this year's gains prove largely temporary). Given how low JKHY's discount rate already is — a 0.55 beta and near-debt-free balance sheet already produce one of the cheapest WACCs in this series — the fact that the market isn't paying up for the base case is notable: a genuinely below-average P/E multiple, on a record year, for a company with Jack Henry's switching-cost moat, is either a rational discount for a real multi-year margin-reinvestment cycle, or a mispricing of a durable compounder that a mechanical DCF, using management's own guidance, would resolve in the stock's favor.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC, and JKHY's unusually low beta-derived discount rate makes the base case particularly sensitive to small changes in either input. Jack Henry's non-calendar (July–June) fiscal year means "FY2026" figures reflect a period that ended two months before this page was written, and the just-filed FY2026 10-K was not yet available through structured filing-facts lookups at the time of writing, so balance-sheet and cash-flow figures were cross-verified against the earnings press release and secondary financial-data sources rather than pulled directly from XBRL tags.
