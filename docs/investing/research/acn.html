Business model

Accenture is one of the world's largest professional-services firms, delivering strategy, consulting, technology, and managed-operations services to enterprise and government clients across nearly every industry, staffed by roughly 800,000 people worldwide. Revenue is earned largely through time-and-materials and fixed-fee engagements — an inherently people-intensive, asset-light model, visible in capex that has never exceeded 1% of revenue in any of the last five fiscal years against consistently double-digit FCF margins. In September 2025 Accenture merged its five historical service lines into a single "Reinvention Services" unit built around embedding AI into every client engagement.

The defining challenge for this DCF — a historic re-rating on AI-disruption fears

ACN stock peaked near $291 in January 2026, then collapsed 59% to a $118 low by late June — including an approximately 18% single-day drop on June 18, 2026, its worst day in years, after Q3 FY2026 results. The quarter itself wasn't bad on the surface (revenue +6% to $18.7B, EPS +9% to $3.80), but consulting revenue grew just 1%, new bookings dipped to $19.3B from $19.7B a year earlier, and management trimmed full-year revenue growth guidance to 3–4% (from 3–5%), with roughly a point of that cut attributed to a slowdown in U.S. federal consulting demand. The reaction crystallized a thesis that had been building for months: that generative AI is starting to erode demand for the traditional billable-hours, staff-augmentation model that has been Accenture's core business for decades. Management's response has been a $865M restructuring charge and roughly 11,000 layoffs (~2% of headcount) to exit staff it can't reskill fast enough, while pointing to $5.9B of cumulative GenAI bookings and AI-related revenue that tripled to $2.7B in FY2025 as evidence Accenture is capturing the AI transition rather than losing to it. The stock has since partially recovered to $176.89, but remains about 39% below its January high.

The debt picture

Accenture carries net cash, not net debt: roughly $8.39B of total debt against $10.17B of cash as of May 31, 2026, for a net cash position of about $1.78B ($2.91/share). The company has been steadily buying back stock and paying dividends — diluted share count has fallen from 629M in FY2025 to roughly 612–615M currently. Reported beta is close to the market average (~1.07 per stockanalysis.com), reflecting a large, diversified, blue-chip services franchise even through the recent volatility.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Because the stock's own re-rating spans a wide range of implied futures, each scenario anchors on a different base: the current TTM run-rate (which still reflects most of the pre-selloff growth trajectory), the smoothed 5-year average (spanning the FY2023–2024 growth deceleration and the FY2025 reacceleration), and the FY2024 actual — the low point right before the AI-bookings ramp, used here as a proxy for what "AI caps growth near zero" would look like. Net cash ($1.78B) is added to, not subtracted from, enterprise value. WACC is widened for the more conservative scenarios.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

The market price sits between the bear and conservative cases, roughly 60% of the way from bear toward conservative — meaning that even after the rebound off the June lows, the market is still pricing something closer to "AI structurally caps growth near zero" than to "growth returns to the historical 5-year trend." That's a different pattern than most other names in this series: the market isn't skeptical of an optimistic DCF here, it's pricing something more pessimistic than even the DCF's own conservative case. The base case — TTM-level growth simply continuing — would require the market to revisit something like the $250–290 valuations investors were paying in January 2026, a level abandoned almost overnight in June. Whether that repricing was an overreaction to one soft quarter and a federal-spending headwind, or a correct read that AI is starting to erode the billable-hours consulting model at its core, is exactly the question a mechanical DCF can't settle — it can only show how far apart those two futures are in dollar terms.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC, and here it is at least as sensitive to whether the FY2024 trough or the FY2025–2026 AI-bookings ramp is the more representative base. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
