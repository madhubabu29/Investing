Business model

Scorpio Tankers owns and operates a fleet of 81 product tankers (31 LR2, 36 MR, 14 Handymax, average age 10.2 years) transporting refined petroleum products worldwide. Revenue comes mostly from pool and spot-market employment plus some time and bareboat charters, which means earnings track prevailing tanker freight rates directly rather than being locked in by long-term contracts.

The defining challenge for this DCF — rate-cycle volatility

STNG's FCF swings with the tanker freight cycle, not a smooth operating trend. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine rerouted global oil product trade onto longer voyages, spiking demand for tanker-days: revenue jumped 189% and FCF went from $26.2M (2021) to $734.9M (2022) almost overnight. Rates have been normalizing down since — FCF fell each year through 2025 to $416.8M — but the cycle isn't purely one-directional: Q2 2026 net income jumped to $387.5M from $73.5M a year earlier as spot rates firmed again (LR2 averaging $80,000/day, MR $53,000/day in the pool). Any single historical year is a poor proxy for a "normal" run-rate here.

The debt picture

As of Scorpio's most recent balance sheet (Q2 2026, period ended June 30, 2026), the company carried $728.9M of debt against $1.84B of cash — a $1.11B net cash position (about $24.40/share). That's a dramatically stronger position than year-end 2025: the FY2025 20-F showed only $151.9M of net cash ($600.1M debt vs. $752.0M cash, about $3.32/share) — roughly a seventh the size. The jump reflects the strong Q2 2026 earnings rebound described above flowing straight into cash. WACC is roughly 7.0% (GuruFocus), though estimates across sources range from 6.2% to 8.65% depending on methodology. Note the company has also just committed to new capex: four scrubber-fitted tankers (delivery 2029–2030) and a minority stake in a joint venture ordering eight scrubber-fitted VLCCs — future cash outflows not reflected in the balance sheet snapshot below.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Given how much a single year's FCF can mislead for a spot-market shipper, each scenario anchors on a different FCF base: the 5-year average (which spans both the 2022 spike and the 2025 trough), the FY2025 actual, and a bear case assuming rates revert most of the way back toward pre-2022, structurally oversupplied levels. Every scenario adds the most recent (Q2 2026) net cash figure of $1.11B and divides by 45.48M shares. Using the much smaller year-end 2025 net cash figure instead ($151.9M, about $3.32/share) would pull every intrinsic value estimate down by roughly $21/share.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

Even the bear case — flat $150M FCF, a level well below any year since the 2022 rate spike, discounted at 9.5% — comes in only 20% below today's price. The market is pricing STNG more pessimistically than a "rates fully revert to pre-2022 norms" scenario, which makes sense for a spot-market shipping company: investors typically apply a heavy discount to peak or even mid-cycle cash flow because the next down-cycle is a matter of when, not if, and new capex commitments (the VLCC JV, the four new tankers) will consume some of that net-cash cushion rather than return it to shareholders. On pure trailing financials STNG looks statistically cheap; the market is pricing the shipping cycle, not the last five years of actuals.

Caveat: DCF is a poor natural fit for a spot-market shipping company — FCF isn't a smooth, compounding series here, it's a rate cycle. The three scenarios above are an attempt to bracket that cycle, not a forecast of it. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
