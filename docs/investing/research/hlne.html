Business model

Hamilton Lane is a specialized private-markets investment manager: it earns recurring management and advisory fees on assets it manages or advises across separate accounts, commingled and specialized funds, and semi-liquid "Evergreen" retail products, plus incentive (carry) fees when funds hit performance hurdles. It doesn't hold the underlying private-equity assets on its own balance sheet at scale — this is a fee-generating, capital-light business, visible in capex that never exceeds $19M in any of the last five fiscal years against $100M+ of annual operating cash flow. Combined assets under management and under advisement crossed $1 trillion in the quarter ended June 2026 ($146B AUM + $914B AUA), and the blended fee rate has been rising as the mix shifts toward higher-fee specialized and Evergreen strategies.

The defining challenge for this DCF — incentive fees are lumpy, and FCF doesn't always track revenue

Revenue has grown almost every year (FY2022 to FY2026: $367.9M to $759.0M), but FCF has not grown in a straight line — FY2024 was a trough at $109.8M (a 19.8% margin, roughly half of the surrounding years) despite revenue still climbing 4.7% that year, a sign that working-capital and incentive-fee-timing effects can swing free cash flow independently of the top line. The most recent quarter (Q1 FY2027, ended June 2026) makes the same point sharply: revenue jumped 56% year-over-year to $275.3M, driven by a 170% surge in incentive fees tied mainly to the quarterly crystallization of performance fees from the U.S. Private Assets Evergreen Fund — but operating cash flow for that same quarter actually fell 40% year-over-year, because crystallized incentive fees get accrued as revenue before they're collected in cash. Trailing-twelve-month FCF (through June 2026) is $366.8M, but a meaningful slice of that reflects one large, lumpy crystallization event rather than a steady, repeatable run-rate — the same caution that applies to any manager whose incentive fees depend on periodic performance hurdles rather than a flat percentage of AUM.

The debt picture

Unlike most companies in this series, Hamilton Lane carries net cash, not net debt: $274.2M of debt against $349.7M of cash and restricted cash as of June 30, 2026, for a net cash position of $75.5M ($1.36/share). Debt has been trending down (from $288.6M a year earlier). Reported beta runs from 1.14 (Google Finance) to 1.29 (TradingView), moderately above the market average — consistent with a fee-based business whose incentive-fee income is geared to private-market performance and fundraising cycles.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Because incentive fees make a single year's FCF an unreliable base, each scenario anchors on a different starting point: the current TTM run-rate (which leans into the recent Evergreen crystallization), the smoothed 5-year average (which nets out both the FY2024 trough and any one-off incentive-fee spikes), and the FY2024 actual (the most recent full-year low, reflecting a period with minimal incentive-fee tailwind). Since Hamilton Lane carries net cash rather than net debt, it is added to — not subtracted from — the discounted enterprise value. Per-share values use the combined Class A and Class B share count (55.53M, as reported May 19, 2026), treating Class B — economically exchangeable 1-for-1 into Class A — as fully converted, consistent with how the company itself presents per-share metrics.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

The market price sits well above the conservative case and just below the base case — the market appears to be pricing continued double-digit growth in fee-earning AUM and the blended fee rate roughly at the pace of the last twelve months, not a reversion to the smoothed 5-year average. That's a defensible bet given the $1 trillion AUM-plus-AUA milestone and the structural growth of private markets, including the newer Evergreen retail products that keep growing fee-earning AUM even between incentive-fee crystallization events. But it leaves little room for error if the TTM base itself is overstated by one large, lumpy incentive-fee quarter rather than reflecting a repeatable run-rate — precisely the FY2024-style air pocket the conservative and bear cases are built to bracket. An investor here is effectively underwriting that Hamilton Lane's incentive-fee lumpiness smooths out into durable growth rather than repeating the FY2024 pattern of a good year with disappointing cash conversion.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC, and for a fee-based manager with large incentive-fee swings it is at least as sensitive to which historical period is chosen as the starting base. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
