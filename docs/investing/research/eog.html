Business model

EOG Resources is a multi-basin U.S. exploration-and-production company built around three "foundational" plays: the oil-weighted Delaware Basin (Permian), the Eagle Ford in South Texas, and — since the $5.6 billion all-cash-and-debt acquisition of Encino Acquisition Partners closed in 2025 — a combined 1.1 million net acres in the natural-gas-and-NGL-rich Utica Shale of Ohio, more than doubling EOG's prior Utica position and adding roughly 275,000 boe/d of pro forma production. On top of the domestic base, EOG runs legacy natural gas and oil operations in Trinidad and is exporting its unconventional operating playbook internationally, with first crude-oil production from an early-stage exploration play in the United Arab Emirates in June 2026 (roughly 500 bbl/d in its first quarter) and an intermittent, conflict-affected program in Bahrain. On a proved-reserves basis the company is still oil-and-liquids-led — about 35% crude/condensate, 27% NGLs, and 38% natural gas — but Encino pushed the gas share of the portfolio meaningfully higher than it was before the deal.

The defining challenge for this DCF — a 2025 trough, a $5.6B gas bet, and a beta that undersells the risk

FY2025 was EOG's soft year: revenue slipped to $22.65B and FCF fell to $3.45B (a 15.2% FCF margin, the worst of the last five years) as oil and gas prices cooled and the company absorbed the financing and integration costs of closing the Encino acquisition mid-year. 2026 has been a sharp rebound — TTM FCF through June 30 is $6.61B, nearly double the FY2025 trough, helped by WTI holding in the low-$80s, a full contribution from Encino's Utica volumes, and management's decision to reallocate 2026's flat $6.5B capital budget away from the dry-gas Dorado asset and toward oil-weighted Delaware and Utica acreage (2026 guidance: oil production +5% year-over-year, total company volumes +14%). The strategic bet embedded in Encino is that a third foundational play — priced mostly off Henry Hub and NGL benchmarks rather than WTI — diversifies EOG's cash flows away from pure oil-price risk. Whether that diversification actually reduces risk, or simply swaps one commodity's cycle for another, is central to how much confidence to place in any single year's FCF as a DCF anchor.

The debt picture

EOG carries one of the strongest balance sheets among large E&Ps: $4.91B of cash against $7.93B of total debt (debt-to-total-capitalization of 20%) as of June 30, 2026, for net debt of just $3.02B ($5.76/share) — and no borrowings drawn on its $3.0B revolving credit facility. The Encino deal was funded with $3.5B of new debt plus $2.1B of cash on hand, and management has continued paying that down alongside record shareholder returns: $2.8B returned to shareholders in the first half of 2026 ($1.05B in dividends, $1.75B in buybacks) under a framework targeting at least 70% of annual free cash flow returned to shareholders, with $11.7B still available under the repurchase authorization at quarter-end. The regular dividend — raised 5% when the Encino deal was announced — has not been cut in 28 years. Reported beta is an extremely low 0.28 (even lower than fellow E&P name RRC's already-unusual 0.42 on this site), which pulls the raw CAPM cost of equity down to 6.1% (4.7% risk-free rate + 0.28 × 5% equity risk premium). Beta measures co-movement with the broad market, not commodity-price risk, so a number this low for a company whose FCF swung from $3.45B to a $6.61B run-rate inside 18 months should be treated with real caution — see the caveat below.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Given how much a single year can mislead for a commodity-cyclical producer, each scenario anchors on a different historical base: the current TTM run-rate (leaning into the 2026 price recovery and the now-full Encino contribution), the smoothed 5-year average (which spans both the 2022 price spike and the 2025 trough), and the FY2025 actual (the most recent full-year low). Every scenario subtracts net debt ($3.02B) and divides by 524.53M shares. WACC is widened by hand for the conservative and bear cases rather than trusting the raw 0.28 beta throughout, given how understated the base-case discount rate looks next to EOG's actual cash-flow volatility.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

The base case's +135% gap to market price is not a credible "the market is wrong" signal — it's a symptom of feeding a genuinely elevated TTM cash-flow run-rate into a discount rate low enough (6.1%) that even a modest 2% terminal growth rate leaves a razor-thin 4.1-point spread between WACC and terminal growth, which mechanically explodes the terminal value to roughly 33x the tenth-year FCF. The conservative case — the 5-year average FCF discounted at a more defensible, hand-widened 7.1% — lands almost exactly on the market price (+14%), suggesting the market is pricing EOG closer to a mid-cycle average producer than to either the current upcycle or the 2025 trough persisting. The real question a DCF can't settle: is 2026's run-rate FCF (full Encino contribution, oil reallocation, WTI in the low-$80s) the new normal, or does it revert toward the 5-year average once the oil-price cycle and integration tailwinds fade? The bear case (-47%) is what a return to 2025-style pricing, with no growth, looks like.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC, and that sensitivity is magnified here because EOG's raw 5-year beta (0.28) is unusually low even by the standards of this site's other commodity-producer pages (RRC's 0.42 was already flagged as suspect) — a reminder that beta captures co-movement with the broad market, not commodity-price risk, and understates it badly for a company whose FCF nearly doubled in 18 months. The base case's discount rate is used as reported by convention, but its output should be read with that mismatch firmly in mind; WACC is widened by hand for the conservative and bear cases rather than trusting the raw beta uniformly. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
