Business model

Range Resources is a pure-play Appalachian Basin producer, concentrated in the liquids-rich Marcellus Shale of southwestern Pennsylvania. It is one of the largest natural gas liquids (NGL) producers and exporters in the U.S., selling ethane and propane into international markets alongside dry gas. Unlike oil-weighted E&Ps, RRC's economics hinge almost entirely on Henry Hub natural gas and Mont Belvieu NGL benchmark pricing — both notoriously volatile, driven by weather, storage levels, and, increasingly, LNG export capacity — not on production growth, which management has kept roughly flat-to-modestly-growing for years off a 30+ year Marcellus inventory position.

The defining challenge for this DCF — two gas-price cycles in five years

FY2022 was a genuine price spike: Russia's invasion of Ukraine triggered a European energy crisis that pulled on global LNG cargoes and lifted domestic Henry Hub pricing too, and RRC's FCF nearly quadrupled to $1.41B (a 34% FCF margin, extreme even by E&P standards). Gas prices then crashed through 2023 and 2024 on mild winters and a domestic storage glut, and FCF fell to $406M and then $374M — a trough barely above 2021's pre-spike level. Now, in 2025–2026, a different and more structural driver is pushing prices back up: U.S. LNG export capacity is ramping hard (management points to LNG demand reaching 24 Bcf/d by the end of 2027), and a new demand source — AI data-center power generation — is pulling incremental gas demand into a market that wasn't sized for it. Trailing-twelve-month FCF (through June 2026) is $726.5M, already ahead of all of FY2025, and Q2 2026 management raised full-year natural gas and NGL price guidance while reporting record production of 2.3 Bcfe/d, guided to 2.5 Bcfe/d by year-end.

The debt picture

RRC paid down $337M of debt in the first half of 2026 alone, cutting long-term debt from $1.70B at the end of FY2024 to $867.1M by June 30, 2026, against essentially no cash on hand. Net debt is a modest $3.71/share. The company also returned $489M of enterprise value to shareholders year-to-date through 2026 — $105M in buybacks and $47M in dividends — funded by the current cash flow upcycle. Reported 5-year beta is unusually low for a commodity producer (0.42 per both Yahoo Finance and Google Finance), which pulls the raw CAPM cost of equity down; see the caveat below on why that number is treated cautiously here.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Given how sharply a single year can mislead for a gas-price cyclical, each scenario anchors on a different base: the current TTM run-rate (leaning into the LNG/AI-demand upcycle), the smoothed 5-year average (which spans both the 2022 spike and the 2023–2024 trough), and the FY2024 actual (the most recent full-year low before this year's recovery). Every scenario subtracts net debt ($866.8M) and divides by 233.68M shares. WACC is widened for the more conservative scenarios rather than relying on the raw 0.42 beta throughout — see caveat.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

The market price sits almost exactly on top of the conservative case — the 5-year-average FCF, discounted at a wider, risk-adjusted WACC. That's a coherent read: the market is pricing RRC as a mid-cycle gas producer, giving essentially no credit for the 2025–2026 upcycle persisting, but also not pricing in a return to the 2023–2024 trough. Whether that's the right call comes down to a single question a DCF can't answer: is the current LNG-export-plus-AI-power demand story structural enough to hold TTM-level FCF for years, or does it repeat 2022's pattern, where an external shock spiked prices and then new supply — Appalachia has 30+ years of low-cost inventory that management is eager to develop — brought them back down? The base case (+74% to market) assumes the former; the bear case (−63%) assumes gas prices revert to the 2023–2024 malaise.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC, and for a gas-price cyclical it is at least as sensitive to which historical period is chosen as the starting base. RRC's raw 5-year beta (0.42) implies an unusually low CAPM cost of equity (6.8%) for a company whose cash flows swing 4x peak-to-trough — a reminder that beta measures co-movement with the broad market, not commodity-price risk, so it understates the risk in a name like this. The WACC is widened by hand for the conservative and bear cases rather than trusting the raw beta uniformly. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
