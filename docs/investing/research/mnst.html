Business model

Monster Beverage makes and markets energy drinks — Monster Energy, Reign, Bang, NOS, Full Throttle, Burn, Predator, and others acquired via a 2015 brand swap with Coca-Cola — sold through a global bottler and distributor network. Coca-Cola owns roughly 19.5% of Monster (a stake that has drifted up over time as Monster's buybacks shrink the share count) and is its preferred global distribution partner, with two board seats, giving Monster access to Coca-Cola's worldwide bottling infrastructure for international expansion while staying operationally independent.

What makes this DCF different from most others on this site

This is one of the steadiest, highest-quality FCF profiles covered here — no cyclicality crash, no capex lumpiness, no balance-sheet drama. FCF margin has expanded from 20.1% to 23.7% over five years (aside from a 2022 dip tied to input-cost inflation and logistics), revenue growth has stayed roughly double-digit most years (with a softer 4.9% in 2024), and capex is a rounding error against FCF — just 1.6% of FCF in FY2025. There's no "which year is normal" judgment call to make; FY2025 actuals are a reasonable base on their own.

The debt picture

Monster retired its remaining debt during 2025 — down from $375M at FY2024 year-end to $0 by June 30, 2025, and it has stayed there since. As of the most recent quarter (June 30, 2026), Monster holds $2.19B of cash plus $1.23B of short-term investments against zero debt: a $3.42B net cash position ($3.49/share). WACC estimates are unusually tight across sources (7.2%–7.79%), reflecting the stock's low volatility relative to most names on this site.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

Even the base case — 10% growth decaying to 6%, at Monster's own low 7.79% discount rate — falls 24% short of today's price. That's a real but far more modest gap than the AI-supercycle names on this site, and there's a specific, plausible explanation the DCF doesn't capture: Monster is a long-rumored acquisition target, with Coca-Cola already holding a ~19.5% stake and board seats. Stocks with credible takeover optionality often trade at a persistent premium to standalone DCF value. The market may also simply be underwriting a longer runway of double-digit growth than the 10-year decaying-growth structure used here, given Monster's international footprint is still small relative to Coca-Cola's own distribution reach.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC — a 1% change in either can swing intrinsic value by 20–30%. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
