Business model

Lululemon designs and sells premium athletic apparel — leggings, joggers, tops — mostly through company-owned stores and e-commerce, with a North America base plus growing (though recently slowing) international exposure, especially China.

The defining challenge for this DCF — a real operational downturn

Unlike most names covered here, this isn't just a valuation re-rating — it's a documented operating deterioration. Americas comparable sales have declined for five straight quarters (down 5% in the most recent quarter), the company cut its FY2026 revenue guidance to roughly flat to down 1%, and margins are being squeezed from two directions: tariffs (a 2.8 percentage-point hit) and rising promotional discounting as competition intensifies from Alo Yoga and Vuori, compounded by product-quality missteps (notably, see-through leggings). The result shows up directly in FCF: down from $1,644.3M two fiscal years ago to just $921.7M in the most recently reported fiscal year (ended February 1, 2026), even though revenue still grew 4.9% — margin compression, not a sales collapse, is doing the damage so far. The stock is down roughly 43–46% in 2026, and the company is also mid-CEO-transition.

The debt picture

Lululemon remains debt-free with $1.51B of cash ($13.97/share) — real staying power to work through the downturn without financial distress. This is an operational and competitive problem, not a balance-sheet one. WACC estimates are currently unusually low (7.5%–7.83%) given the stock's historically low beta; the 10-year median (9.05%) is used as the base case here to avoid understating the discount rate during a period of real business uncertainty.

All three scenarios use the most recent fiscal year's FCF ($921.7M) as the base and differ only on whether the business stabilizes, stays flat, or keeps deteriorating. Every scenario adds net cash ($1.51B) and divides by 108.44M shares.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

The market price sits close to the conservative case — essentially flat FCF forever, roughly matching the company's own guidance — rather than pricing a full structural collapse. The P/E has compressed from a historic growth-stock premium to just 9.76x, in line with a mature, no-growth retailer rather than the premium athleisure grower LULU used to be priced as. Given that even the base case (a modest recovery scenario) implies 23% upside, the real question isn't whether LULU looks statistically cheap — it does, even under a no-growth assumption — it's whether the current deterioration is temporary (a fixable tariff shock and product misstep) or structural (permanent share loss to Alo Yoga and Vuori, brand fatigue in a category Lululemon used to dominate). A DCF can bracket that range; it can't answer which side of it is right.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC — a 1% change in either can swing intrinsic value by 20–30%. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
