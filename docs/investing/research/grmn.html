Business model

Garmin makes GPS-enabled devices and software across five reporting segments, diversified in a way most single-product names in this series are not: Fitness (running/multisport watches, activity trackers — 33% of FY2025's $7.25B revenue, up 33% YoY), Outdoor (handhelds, adventure watches, golf devices — 28%), Marine (chartplotters, sonar, autopilot — 16%), Aviation (avionics and flight instruments for general aviation, business jets and government/defense — 14%), and Auto OEM (embedded infotainment and navigation systems sold to automakers, chiefly BMW today with Mercedes-Benz starting in 2027 — 9%). Aviation and Marine carry Garmin's highest margins; Fitness is now both the largest segment and the fastest-growing, powered by a run of advanced wearable launches that made Garmin the biggest smartwatch-market-share gainer of any non-Apple brand, pulling it level with Samsung at roughly 15% global share in Q2 2026.

The defining challenge for this DCF — a wearables boom, an Auto OEM air pocket, and a rich multiple

Two forces are pulling Garmin's growth profile in opposite directions right now. Fitness is on a tear — revenue up 33% in FY2025 and 25% in Q2 2026 — as Garmin's advanced wearables take share from Apple, Samsung, and smaller specialists like Oura and Whoop in a smartwatch market that itself grew only mid-single digits. At the same time, Auto OEM — the smallest segment at 9% of revenue — is entering a deliberate air pocket: the BMW infotainment program that has carried the segment is rolling off peak volumes through 2026, pushing the business back toward an operating loss in the second half, before a much larger Mercedes-Benz program is scheduled to ramp starting in early 2027. Management's raised FY2026 guidance (~$8.05B revenue, 59.7% gross margin, 27.0% operating margin, $10.00 pro forma EPS) essentially bets that Fitness's share gains and Aviation/Marine's steady growth more than offset that one segment's planned trough. The market has rewarded the wearables story specifically: the stock has returned roughly 202% over the past three years and now trades around 26x–30x trailing earnings, versus a 10-year average near 21x and a 5-year average near 23x — a premium that assumes the Fitness momentum, not just the eventual Auto OEM recovery, keeps going.

The debt picture

Garmin carries no debt and holds about $4.4B of cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities as of June 27, 2026 ($2.33B cash, $0.33B current and $1.70B noncurrent securities) — a net cash position of $22.66/share. It pays a $4.20/share annual dividend (four $1.05 quarterly installments, $376M paid in H1 2026) and repurchased $81.6M of stock in H1 2026 against a $448M authorization running through December 2028 — a buyback pace that is modest relative to the cash pile, so capital return isn't stretching the balance sheet. Beta is low at 0.87, consistent with a diversified, dividend-paying, debt-free profile; using CAPM (4.7% risk-free rate, 5% equity risk premium) puts the cost of equity — and, with no debt, the WACC — at roughly 9.1%, one of the lowest discount rates in this series.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Each scenario anchors on a different FCF base. The base case uses the TTM run-rate and credits growth roughly in line with management's own newly-raised FY2026 guidance before decaying; the conservative case starts from FY2025's actual FCF and assumes growth normalizes as the Fitness share-gain run matures and Auto OEM's 2027 recovery only partly offsets its 2026 trough; the bear case starts from the smoothed 5-year average FCF — which still carries the weight of 2021–2022's pre-wearables-boom margins — and assumes low-single-digit growth, treating the current cycle as largely cyclical rather than a durable company-wide re-rating. Every scenario adds net cash ($4.37B) and divides by 192.85M shares.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

Every scenario here falls short of the market price — including the base case, which already credits management's own newly-raised FY2026 growth guidance and a below-market 9.1% discount rate reflecting Garmin's genuinely low beta and debt-free balance sheet. That echoes ETN and INTU elsewhere in this series: a real, guidance-backed growth story that the market is nonetheless pricing at a multiple (26x–30x earnings, versus a 10-year average near 21x) that a mechanical DCF can't reach without either far higher long-run growth than the Fitness segment's current 25%–33% pace can plausibly sustain company-wide, or a discount rate implausibly below the CAPM estimate for a 0.87-beta company. The bet embedded in today's price is less about the DCF's growth-rate assumptions and more about duration: that Garmin's wearables share gains are a permanent re-rating of the whole company, not a multi-year cyclical high before Apple, Samsung, or a still-emerging category leader like Oura or Whoop claws share back.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC. Auto OEM's 2026 trough and 2027 Mercedes-Benz ramp aren't modeled as a separate cash-flow path — they're folded into the blended, company-wide growth rate, which likely understates how lumpy that one segment's swing could be in either direction. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
