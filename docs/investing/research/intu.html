Business model

Intuit is a financial software company spanning TurboTax (DIY tax prep, roughly 70% of the market), QuickBooks (small business accounting, over 80% share of SMB accounting), Credit Karma (consumer credit and financial-product recommendations), and Mailchimp (email marketing, acquired 2021). FY2022's 32.1% revenue jump reflects the Credit Karma (closed Dec 2020) and Mailchimp (closed Nov 2021) acquisitions layering onto the base business.

What's different about this one — growth and margin expanding together

Unlike most names on this site, INTU shows high, stable FCF margins (29%–34% every year) alongside accelerating revenue growth: 12.9% (FY2023) → 13.3% (FY2024) → 15.6% (FY2025). This isn't a turnaround or a cyclical bet — it's a steady compounder. The risks the company itself flags are nearer-term: Mailchimp softness, international headwinds, less pricing uplift expected in FY2026, and Credit Karma's inherent cyclicality (its revenue is largely lender-advertising and lead-gen, tied to credit demand). The more interesting longer-term threat is AI: a new wave of AI tax and bookkeeping agents (e.g., Perplexity's "Computer for Taxes") threatens to commoditize the DIY tax-prep category Intuit has long dominated — even as the IRS's own free "Direct File" program was discontinued ahead of the 2026 filing season, removing one competitive threat just as a different one emerges. Intuit is racing to embed its own AI (Intuit Assist, built on a proprietary "Tax Knowledge Engine") directly into TurboTax, QuickBooks, and Credit Karma to stay ahead of that shift.

The debt picture

Intuit carries $6.16B of debt (largely tied to the Credit Karma and Mailchimp acquisitions) against $4.68B of cash — a modest $1.48B net debt position, easily covered by a single year of FCF. WACC estimates are unusually tight across sources (7.88%–8.6%), reflecting the stock's relatively low volatility.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

This is the tightest spread between market price and any DCF scenario on this site: even the bear case — growth decaying from 4% to 2%, at a 10% discount rate — lands just 6% below today's price. Turned around, that means the market is pricing INTU close to a scenario that assumes meaningfully decelerating growth, while the base and conservative cases (built on continuing something closer to the recent 12–16% trend) imply 52–145% upside. The likely explanation: with TurboTax already near 70% DIY tax share and QuickBooks above 80% of SMB accounting, the easy new-customer growth is mostly gone — further growth increasingly depends on premium mix-shift, AI monetization, and Credit Karma/Mailchimp cross-sell, all of which are inherently harder to extrapolate with confidence than straight customer-count growth. The market may simply not trust a decade of 12%+ compounding from here, even though the last three years delivered exactly that.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC — a 1% change in either can swing intrinsic value by 20–30%. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
