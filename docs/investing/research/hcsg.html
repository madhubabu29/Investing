Business model

Healthcare Services Group outsources two unglamorous but essential functions for nursing homes and other long-term-care facilities: housekeeping/laundry/linen services (the Environmental Services, or EVS, segment — $213.2M of Q2 2026 revenue at a 13.3% segment margin) and food purchasing, meal preparation, menu development and dietician consulting (the Dietary segment — $257.6M of Q2 2026 revenue at a 7.5% margin). Founded in 1976 and headquartered in Bensalem, Pennsylvania, HCSG employs roughly 36,000 people, most of them front-line housekeeping and dietary staff working on-site at client facilities under multi-year service contracts that typically include contractual pass-throughs for food and wage inflation. No single customer has represented more than 10% of consolidated revenue in recent years, and management reports customer retention above 90% with a "robust and growing" sales pipeline — but the client base itself is the structural issue this DCF has to grapple with: HCSG's revenue ultimately depends on skilled nursing and long-term-care operators whose own economics run on thin, state-set Medicaid margins.

The defining challenge for this DCF — client credit risk from a chronically thin-margin customer base

HCSG's history is punctuated by episodes where a nursing-home operator client becomes unable to pay, and the clearest recent example is Genesis Healthcare's Chapter 11 filing on July 9, 2025. HCSG served 164 Genesis facilities and, rather than exiting the relationship, had historically extended credit to Genesis in the form of notes receivable — a pattern of accommodating distressed but strategically important customers that has recurred across HCSG's history. When Genesis's position deteriorated, HCSG recorded a non-cash charge of roughly $0.62/share in Q2 2025 in anticipation, followed by another $0.03–$0.04/share in Q3 2025 after the filing, and took a 100% allowance against both the $50.4M of accounts receivable and $20.4M of notes receivable still outstanding from Genesis as of June 30, 2026. Total bad debt expense for the first nine months of 2025 reached $63.9M — a huge swing versus HCSG's own stated historical average bad debt run-rate of 1%–1.5% of revenue — and it is the direct explanation for why FY2024's FCF ($24.5M) and FY2025's FCF ($139.2M) look so different: 2025's operating cash flow benefited from bad-debt provisions unwinding as non-cash add-backs even as net income absorbed the P&L hit. This is not a one-off: bad debt provisions also spiked to $32.0M in 2022 (versus $10.5M in 2021) amid a broader wave of post-COVID skilled-nursing-facility distress, and that episode is what triggered HCSG's Board to suspend its quarterly dividend in February 2023 — ending a streak of 77 consecutive annual dividend increases dating back to the dividend's 2003 initiation — "given the prolonged industry recovery" and to preserve financial flexibility. By Q2 2026, management characterized bad debt expense as back below that 1%–1.5% historical band, and a December 2025 CMS interim final rule repealing the Biden-era nursing-home minimum-staffing mandate (saving operators an estimated $1.75B/year in labor costs) is a tailwind for client solvency going forward — but the underlying dependency on Medicaid/Medicare reimbursement adequacy at the client level hasn't gone away, and won't.

The debt picture

HCSG carries no funded debt and has full, undrawn access to a $300M revolving credit facility. It ended Q2 2026 (June 30, 2026) with $200.9M of cash and marketable securities against $0 of debt — a net cash position of $200.9M, or $2.93/share on 68.63M shares outstanding. Rather than restore the dividend suspended in 2023, management has returned capital through buybacks: a $50M, 12-month repurchase program launched in 2025 was completed five months ahead of schedule (over $60M returned to shareholders for the year), and a new $75M, 12-month program followed in early 2026, with $20.9M repurchased in Q2 2026 alone. Beta is low at 0.82 — unsurprising for a low-growth, contract-revenue services business — and applying CAPM (4.7% risk-free rate, 5% equity risk premium) puts the cost of equity, and with no debt the WACC, at roughly 8.8%. That low beta reflects how little HCSG's stock co-moves with the broader market; it does not capture the idiosyncratic, recurring risk of a single nursing-home-operator bankruptcy blowing a hole in a given year's receivables.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Each scenario anchors on a different FCF base to bracket how much of the Genesis-recovery-driven FY2025/TTM cash-flow surge is durable. The base case uses the TTM run-rate and assumes mid-single-digit growth roughly in line with management's reaffirmed FY2026 guidance, on the view that bad debt expense holding near its historical 1%–1.5%-of-revenue band is the new normal rather than a temporary reprieve. The conservative case starts from FY2025's actual FCF and tempers growth further, treating some of the bad-debt-provision unwind as non-repeating. The bear case starts from the smoothed 5-year average FCF — which blends 2022's bad-debt-driven negative FCF year against 2025's bad-debt-recovery year — and assumes minimal growth, treating HCSG's cash flow as inherently volatile and hostage to the next distressed client rather than steadily compounding. Every scenario adds net cash ($200.9M) and divides by 68.63M shares.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

Every scenario except the bear case values HCSG well above its $22.42 market price — even the conservative case, built on FY2025's already-realized FCF and modest single-digit growth, comes in 41% above where the stock trades. That gap is the mirror image of the usual "growth story priced ahead of the DCF" pattern seen elsewhere in this series: here, a mechanical CAPM-derived 8.8% WACC — built entirely from HCSG's low 0.82 beta — understates the discount rate the market is actually applying, because beta measures co-movement with the broader market, not the idiosyncratic risk that a Genesis-sized client goes bankrupt and wipes out tens of millions in receivables in a single quarter. The market's ~13x trailing P/E and roughly 10x TTM-FCF multiple reflect two decades of institutional memory: HCSG's cash flow has now swung from a $13.4M FCF deficit (2022) to a $139.2M surplus (2025) inside a single client-credit cycle, and pricing it as though that swing won't recur requires more conviction in the durability of the current low-bad-debt environment than the reimbursement dynamics underlying HCSG's entire client base can really promise.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC, and for HCSG specifically, the FCF base is sensitive to which point in the multi-year bad-debt cycle you sample — the three bases used here span more than a 3x range. The CAPM-derived WACC used for the base case likely understates HCSG's true discount rate because beta doesn't capture client-concentration credit-loss tail risk. Genesis's Chapter 11 case remains open and could still generate further recoveries or losses not modeled here. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
