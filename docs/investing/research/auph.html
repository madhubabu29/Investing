Business model

Aurinia is a commercial-stage biopharmaceutical company built almost entirely around one approved drug: LUPKYNIS (voclosporin), an oral calcineurin inhibitor that became the first FDA-approved oral therapy for adult active lupus nephritis in January 2021. Lupus nephritis is a kidney-damaging complication of systemic lupus erythematosus and a leading cause of dialysis and transplant among lupus patients if left inadequately treated. LUPKYNIS is sold almost entirely in the US through a specialty-pharmacy network; a Japanese approval (September 2024) and other ex-US rights held by Otsuka contribute a small stream of license, collaboration and royalty revenue on top of net product sales. Net product sales have compounded from $45.6M in the drug's 2021 launch year to $283.1M in FY2025, and management guides to $305–315M for full-year 2026 (11–15% growth), plus a small early-stage pipeline: aritinercept (AUR200), a Phase 1 dual BAFF/APRIL inhibitor, and — since May 2026 — zetomipzomib, a Phase 2 immunoproteasome inhibitor in development for lupus nephritis and autoimmune hepatitis, acquired along with the rest of Kezar Life Sciences (see below).

The defining challenge for this DCF — a single-product cash machine, a new activist-investor CEO, and a related-party acquisition

Two things make Aurinia unusual among the names in this series. First, essentially all of its cash flow still comes from one drug, and the operating leverage on that single product has been dramatic: the FCF margin went from -346% in 2021 (pre-launch cash burn) to +48% in FY2025 and +56% on a TTM basis, a run of margin expansion that is unlikely to keep compounding at the same rate once R&D spending on the newly acquired pipeline ramps up. Second, governance and capital allocation are now concentrated in one person. Activist investor Kevin Tang joined Aurinia's board as chairman in 2024, became CEO in 2026, and then used that dual role to complete a deal his own investment vehicle, Tang Capital Partners, had tried and failed to strike directly with Kezar in 2024: an all-cash acquisition of Kezar Life Sciences, valued at just over $50M ($6.955/share plus a contingent value right), announced March 30, 2026 and closed May 11, 2026. The deal is small relative to Aurinia's $443M cash pile, but it commits company resources to funding an earlier-stage, higher-risk pipeline just as Aurinia's single approved product has turned into a highly profitable, royalty-like cash generator — and it was struck by a chairman-turned-CEO who had a prior personal stake in the target.

The debt picture

Aurinia carries no debt. As of June 30, 2026 it held $443.1M of cash, cash equivalents and investments — up from $398.0M at 2025 year-end even after repurchasing 5.0M shares for $74.9M during H1 2026 — against $0 of debt, for a net cash position of $443.1M ($3.33/share). Beta is elevated at 1.40, more typical of a single-drug biotech than a diversified pharma; applying CAPM (4.7% risk-free rate, 5% equity risk premium) puts the cost of equity — and, since there is no debt, the WACC — at roughly 11.7%. A March 2026 settlement with Teva pushed the earliest possible date for a generic competitor to voclosporin out to December 7, 2036, meaningfully de-risking the multi-year terminal-value assumption this DCF depends on, though patent litigation against several other generic filers (DifGen, Dr. Reddy's, Hikma, Lotus/Sandoz, Zydus) remains unresolved.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Each scenario anchors on a different FCF base to bracket how much of the last two years' margin expansion is durable. The base case takes the TTM run-rate and credits growth broadly consistent with the pace of the last two years before decelerating; the conservative case starts from FY2025's actual FCF and assumes growth moderates as the treated lupus-nephritis population matures and R&D spend on zetomipzomib and aritinercept increases; the bear case starts from FY2024's actual FCF — before the sharpest year of margin expansion — and assumes low-single-digit growth, treating most of 2025–2026's margin gain as not durable. Every scenario adds back net cash ($443.1M) and divides by 132.9M shares.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

Unlike most names in this series, where the market price sits well above every scenario or squarely between the base and bear cases, AUPH's $16.30 price sits just below the conservative case and far above the bear case. That's a specific bet: the market isn't pricing Aurinia as a typical single-product biotech coasting toward an eventual growth wall, and it isn't pricing the base case's continued high-teens growth either. It's pricing something close to the conservative case — LUPKYNIS growth decelerating from its current 20%+ pace toward something closer to double digits, with most of the recent margin expansion holding. Two things could break that read: R&D spend tied to the newly acquired zetomipzomib and aritinercept programs ramps up faster than expected and compresses the FCF margin back toward FY2024 levels (pulling the outcome toward the bear case), or LUPKYNIS penetration into an underdiagnosed lupus-nephritis population keeps running ahead of guidance (pulling the outcome toward the base case). A mechanical DCF can bracket both outcomes but can't tell you which one a newly installed activist-investor CEO — now running both a mature commercial asset and a freshly acquired early-stage pipeline — is more likely to deliver.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC, and for a single-product biotech it is at least as sensitive to how much of the recent FCF-margin expansion is treated as durable — the three FCF bases used here span roughly a 4x range. The Teva settlement reduces near-term genericization risk, but litigation against other generic filers is ongoing and unresolved-outcome risk isn't modeled. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
