Model-based rating: SELL at this price — very high risk

Read this first: on the cash flow as reported, GE Vernova looks fairly priced. Twelve-month free cash flow after share-based pay is $16.8B, which is worth about $684 a share at 10% with no growth, against the $699 price (implied growth 0.2%). That would be a BUY under my growth rule. I did not use it, because $25.1B of the $19.2B of twelve-month operating cash flow is the increase in contract liabilities, which are mostly customer deposits and advance billings on equipment that has not yet been delivered. That cash has to be earned by delivering the equipment, and it stops growing if orders slow. Excluding the increase, the same twelve months show −$8.3B, and every year back to 2024 is negative on that basis (2024 −$1.3B, 2025 −$4.6B).

Rule used for this page (my adaptation, not from any filing): the growth-implied rule is applied to free cash flow after share-based pay excluding the growth in customer deposits. With a negative base, no growth rate makes $699 fair value, so the rating is SELL. The thresholds used elsewhere on this site (BUY at or below 2% implied growth, HOLD 2%–4%, NOT A BUY 4%–5%, SELL above 5%) are my own. At this price, recurring FCF would need to reach about $8.2B a year for the rating to move to NOT A BUY, about $10.0B for HOLD, and about $13.5B for BUY. For comparison, the as-reported twelve-month figure is $16.8B.

What would change this: evidence that cash flow excluding deposit growth turns solidly positive, for example inventory and receivables stopping their growth and cash taxes falling, would move the rating up. If customer deposits keep growing at the current pace, reported FCF stays high and the as-reported view stays at about fair value. Main risk to the rating: the deposit adjustment is harsh in one respect: part of the working-capital build (inventories, receivables) is the cost of growing, and the deposits fund it. I could not separate steady-state working capital from growth. The rating is therefore a statement about how much of the price depends on deposits and growth continuing, not a forecast that they will stop.

What changed in this version

This page was rebuilt using only GE Vernova's SEC filings (the 2025 10-K and the Q3 2026 10-Q). The earlier version used a CAPM-derived cost of capital and an older price, and it did not deduct share-based pay. The filings contain no cost of capital, so values are shown across an 8%–12% range, with the market price used only as a comparison. FCF here is operating cash flow − gross additions to property, plant and equipment and internal-use software − share-based compensation (from the statement of changes in equity).

Debt is low relative to size. Borrowings are $2.8B of senior notes (including $2.6B issued Feb 4, 2026 at 4.25%–5.50%, partly to fund the Prolec GE purchase) plus $271M of finance leases. The $3.0B revolver and $3.0B letter-of-credit facility were undrawn. Cash is $16.8B, but contract liabilities and deferred income are $45.7B, so most of that cash is customers' money that funds work in progress. If you prefer companies with little or no debt, GE Vernova has little financial debt, but the balance sheet is funded by customers.

Method

The value is the present value of a flat perpetual stream of free cash flow (after share-based pay) plus net cash, divided by shares. No growth is assumed in the main table (g = 0). Scenarios A–C use free cash flow exactly as reported; scenario D removes the increase in contract liabilities, which is negative in every period shown. Equity value = FCF ÷ discount rate + cash − debt − noncontrolling interests.

Caveats

  • The rating depends on the deposit adjustment. Without it, the model output would be BUY (implied growth 0.2%), and the HOLD, NOT A BUY and SELL lines would be about $764, $1,056 and $1,289. I judged that headline too dependent on deposit growth to rely on.
  • The balance sheet increase in contract liabilities ($19.8B in nine months) includes the Prolec GE acquisition (consolidated from Feb 2, 2026). I used the cash flow statement figure ($19.5B), which is close, but I did not confirm how acquired balances are treated in it. Twelve-month figures also mix periods with and without Prolec.
  • Cash taxes paid ($2.7B in nine months) and acquisition effects make the latest nine months hard to compare with 2025.
  • Cash includes restricted cash, and most of it is funded by customer deposits, so net cash overstates what is available to shareholders.
  • Finance leases are counted as debt and noncontrolling interests as debt-like; operating leases are not counted.
  • Any growth beyond flat FCF is excluded. GE Vernova has a $196.7B backlog and revenue growing about 22% in 2026, so a business that converts it into recurring cash would be worth more than scenario D shows.
  • The Q3 10-Q I could read did not give a free cash flow discussion, so I used no company-defined free cash flow.

These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice; I am not a financial advisor.
