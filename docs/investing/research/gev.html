Business model

GE Vernova sells the equipment and software that generate, convert, and move electricity, organized into three segments. Power (gas, steam, nuclear, and hydro turbines and their long-tail services business — about 52% of FY2025's $38.1B revenue) is the largest segment and the direct beneficiary of the AI-data-center power buildout: it books multi-year "slot reservation" and firm equipment orders for gas turbines years before the units are built and shipped. Electrification (grid solutions, power conversion, electrification software, and solar/storage — about 25% of revenue, and now including the recently closed Prolec-GE transformer joint-venture acquisition) is the fastest-growing segment, supplying the transformers, switchgear, and grid-connection equipment utilities need to actually interconnect all that new generation and data-center load. Wind (onshore and offshore turbines and blades — about 24% of revenue) is the structural laggard: it has been loss-making since spinoff and remains so, dragged down by U.S. tariff exposure, weak onshore order intake, and costly offshore projects. Customers are overwhelmingly utilities, independent power producers, and grid operators — GE Vernova doesn't sell directly to hyperscalers, but hyperscaler and data-center power demand is the demand driver showing up in utility and IPP gas-turbine orders.

The defining challenge for this DCF — an order book that's exploding faster than the company can build turbines, funding a cash surge that may not repeat

GE Vernova's Q2 2026 numbers are extraordinary by almost any measure: orders of $24.2B (up 88% organically), a backlog that grew $13B sequentially to $176.3B, and revenue up 22% to $11.1B. Gas Power's equipment-plus-slot-reservation backlog reached 116 GW (versus 100 GW just three months earlier), with management now targeting at least 125 GW under contract by year-end 2026 and only about 10 GW of open 2029–2030 production capacity left to sell — evidence that GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (the only three heavy-gas-turbine makers left) are effectively sold out years in advance. Management has responded by raising full-year 2026 revenue guidance to $45.5–46.5B and, more strikingly, free cash flow guidance to $11.5–12.5B — nearly double the $6.5–7.5B it guided to at the start of the year — while lifting Power's organic growth guidance to 18–20%. The catch is that a large share of that cash surge (TTM FCF of $12,438M is roughly 3.4x FY2025's $3,710M) reflects customer deposits and progress payments collected against a backlog that has more than doubled in a year, not yet fully-recognized profit on delivered equipment; Wind, meanwhile, is still guided to roughly $400M of segment EBITDA losses for 2026. The market has re-rated the stock accordingly — up roughly 6.4x from its $142.85 spinoff-day open in April 2024 to $911.93 today, having touched an all-time high of $1,195.94 on July 6, 2026 — on the bet that gas-turbine scarcity pricing and the Electrification build-out are durable, not that this specific pace of cash generation repeats indefinitely.

The debt picture

GE Vernova is a net-cash company. As of June 30, 2026 it held $13.12B of cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash against roughly $2.8B of total borrowings — almost entirely the $2.6B of senior notes (4.250% due 2031, 4.875% due 2036, 5.500% due 2056) it issued in February 2026 to help fund the Prolec-GE transformer acquisition — for net cash of about $10.27B, or $38.57/share, on top of a fully undrawn $6.0B committed credit facility. In December 2025 the board doubled the quarterly dividend to $0.50/share and raised the buyback authorization to $10B from $6B, signaling confidence the cash position can support both continued capital return and the capex needed to expand gas-turbine manufacturing capacity from 20 GW/year today toward a targeted 30 GW/year by 2030. One caveat: this net-cash figure excludes GE Vernova's legacy, non-financial obligations inherited at spinoff — principally an underfunded pension and retiree-benefit plan (roughly $1.3–1.6B net underfunded as of recent fiscal year-ends) that GE Vernova now sponsors outright. Beta is 1.03 — essentially market-average, and GuruFocus's own July 2025 WACC estimate of 10.33% explicitly disclosed it was built on a default beta of 1 because GEV then had under three years of trading history; using the now-observable 1.03 beta in CAPM (4.7% risk-free rate, 5% equity risk premium) gives a base WACC of 9.85%.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Each scenario anchors on a different FCF base to bracket how much of the current cash surge is durable. The base case uses TTM FCF ($12,438M) grown at 8%/4% — roughly tracking management's own newly-raised FY2026 FCF guidance of $11.5–12.5B, which is essentially flat against the TTM run-rate rather than assuming further acceleration. The conservative case anchors on FY2025's actual FCF ($3,710M) — the last full year before the Prolec-GE deal and the backlog's explosive growth — and grows it faster (10%/5%) on the assumption that the backlog does convert into cash over time, just from a less deposit-inflated starting point. The bear case annualizes Q2 2025's FCF ($194M x4 = $776M), the most recent "normal" quarter before the order surge began, and grows it only 3%/1.5%, treating the last twelve months of cash generation as a temporary, order-timing spike rather than a new baseline. Every scenario adds net cash ($10,271M) and divides by 266.33M shares.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

GEV inverts the pattern seen almost everywhere else in this series. For ETN, GRMN, and others, even the bullish base case — crediting management's own raised guidance — falls well short of the market price, implying the market is paying for a permanently higher multiple on top of already-strong growth. Here, the base case lands almost exactly at today's $911.93, because it takes management's own newly-raised FY2026 free-cash-flow guidance ($11.5–12.5B, essentially flat versus the current TTM run-rate) at face value. The uncomfortable question is therefore not about the multiple — it's about the base year. TTM FCF of $12,438M is roughly 3.4x FY2025's $3,710M, and a meaningful share of that jump is customer deposits and progress billings against a backlog that grew from $135B to $176B in barely six months, not steady-state profit on equipment actually delivered. Normalize away that surge — using FY2025's actual FCF or the pre-surge Q2 2025 run-rate as the true baseline — and the stock is 68% to 92% overvalued. The entire bull case rests on whether GE Vernova's backlog conversion, gas-turbine capacity expansion, and Electrification growth can sustain something close to the current, unusually elevated pace of cash generation, rather than reverting toward FY2025's much lower level once the order book's deposit-heavy front end works through the system — all while Wind continues to lose roughly $400M a year.

Caveat: DCF is extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC. This model blends all three segments into one company-wide FCF and growth path — it doesn't separately value Wind's structural losses against Power and Electrification's profits, so the base and conservative cases implicitly assume Wind's roughly $400M annual drag doesn't worsen. GE Vernova's legacy pension and retiree-benefit obligations inherited from GE are real long-term liabilities but are excluded from the net-cash figure above, which covers financial debt only. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
