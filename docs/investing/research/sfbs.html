Business model

ServisFirst is a Birmingham, Alabama-based commercial bank holding company, founded in 2007 by Tom Broughton, operating a single bank subsidiary (ServisFirst Bank) across Alabama, Florida, Georgia, North and South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, and Virginia. The model is deliberately narrow: relationship-driven commercial and correspondent banking (it banks other, smaller community banks) delivered through a lean branch footprint and roughly 666 employees, rather than a retail-heavy, branch-dense strategy. That low-overhead structure is the whole investment thesis in one number: ServisFirst posted a 29.65% efficiency ratio in Q2 2026 (down from 33.46% a year earlier) — a fraction of the 55–65% typical at most regional banks — meaning it spends roughly 30 cents to generate a dollar of revenue versus 55-65 cents at peers. As of June 30, 2026 the bank held $18.35B in total assets, $14.48B in loans (+9.4% year over year) and $14.55B in deposits (+5.0% year over year), funding growth mostly through core deposits rather than brokered funding or FHLB advances (both effectively zero on the balance sheet).

The defining challenge for this DCF — riding the rate-cut cycle, and a credit-quality tick to watch

ServisFirst's earnings this year have been supercharged by the direction of interest-rate cuts, not by underlying volume growth alone. As the Fed's cutting cycle has worked through 2025 and into 2026, ServisFirst's cost of interest-bearing deposits fell from 3.33% to 2.80% (-53bp) year over year — faster than its 6.23% loan yield has come down — which pushed net interest margin from 3.10% to 3.63% (+53bp) and net interest income from $131.7M to $155.6M in Q2 2026 alone. That margin tailwind, combined with the efficiency-ratio discipline above, drove net income up 39.7% year over year and return on average equity to 17.71% (from 14.56%) and return on average assets to 1.91% (from 1.40%). The open question for a 10-year DCF is durability: this is a liability-sensitive balance sheet that gets a one-time margin boost each leg down in rates as deposits reprice faster than loans, and that boost mechanically fades once the cutting cycle ends or reverses. Layered on top, nonperforming assets/total assets more than doubled year over year, from 0.42% to 0.96% — still a low absolute level, but a data point worth watching given ServisFirst's concentration in commercial and commercial real estate lending across Sun Belt markets that have seen aggressive CRE construction. The board's July 2026 two-for-one stock split (record date August 5, distributed August 20) doesn't change any of the economics modeled here — share count and price both adjust proportionally — but it does underline how far a name that IPO'd in 2014 has run.

The debt picture

ServisFirst is, deliberately, one of the least-levered names in this series at the holding-company level. Its only funded parent-level debt is $34.75M of 4.00% Subordinated Notes due October 21, 2030 (issued in a October 2020 private placement); a second tranche, $30.0M of 4.50% Subordinated Notes due 2027, was redeemed in Q4 2025. That $34.75M is the only figure treated as "debt" in this analysis. Two things are deliberately excluded: customer deposits ($14.55B) are the bank's funding base, not a financing choice comparable to corporate borrowing, so they are never netted against cash on this page; and the $1.58B of federal funds purchased on the balance sheet is bank-subsidiary-level overnight wholesale liquidity management, not parent-level capital structure, so it is excluded too. The bank's $1.46B of consolidated cash is held at the ServisFirst Bank subsidiary level and is not freely available to the holding company without a dividend up from the bank (subject to regulatory capital limits), and parent-only cash isn't broken out separately in the data available for this page — so rather than guess, this page reports the $34.75M as a standalone gross debt figure (-$0.32/share on 109.3M post-split shares) instead of netting it against cash it may not actually control. Beta is low at 0.86; with CAPM (4.7% risk-free rate, 5% equity risk premium) that puts cost of equity — used here as WACC, since net income (the FCF proxy, see below) is already an after-interest-expense, levered-equity cash flow — at roughly 9.0%.

DCF inputs and results — three scenarios

Methodology note: this site normally defines FCF as operating cash flow minus capex. For a lending institution like ServisFirst that definition breaks down — operating cash flow is dominated by loan origination/sale timing and deposit flows, and capex (branches, IT) is trivial relative to an $18B balance sheet, so OpCF-minus-capex would mostly measure balance-sheet noise, not economic profit available to shareholders. This page substitutes net income attributable to shareholders as the distributable-earnings proxy instead, a standard practitioner shortcut for bank DCFs (closer in spirit to a dividend-discount or excess-capital model). Each scenario anchors on a different net-income base to bracket how much of the current rate-cut-driven margin expansion is durable. The base case uses the TTM figure, still growing at roughly the recent trend before decelerating; the conservative case starts from FY2024's actual net income — before the sharpest year of margin and efficiency-ratio improvement — and assumes more moderate growth as the rate-cut tailwind fades and credit costs normalize; the bear case starts from FY2023's actual net income, the lowest of the last five years, and assumes low-single-digit growth, treating most of the recent margin and efficiency gains as cyclical rather than structural. Every scenario nets out the $34.75M of holding-company debt and divides by 109.3M shares.

The uncomfortable question the DCF raises

SFBS's $43.20 price sits squarely between the conservative and base cases — not stretched to either extreme the way some names in this series are. That's arguably the market pricing this correctly as a bank whose current 40%-plus earnings growth is real but partly cyclical: it reflects a genuine rate-cut tailwind (deposit costs falling faster than loan yields) and genuine efficiency-ratio discipline (29.65%, best-in-class), but also a margin dynamic that mechanically fades once the Fed stops cutting. A P/E of 14.7x and P/B of 2.4x on a bank generating 17.7% ROE and 1.91% ROA is not an expensive multiple by regional-bank standards — if anything it looks like the market is already discounting some mean reversion in the margin tailwind, which is consistent with the DCF landing between the conservative and base cases rather than at either extreme. The bigger unknown this DCF can't resolve is credit: nonperforming assets doubling year over year, from a low base, in a book concentrated in Southeast commercial and CRE lending, is the kind of thing that looks immaterial until a credit cycle turns and it isn't.

Caveat: this page departs from this site's usual FCF definition — operating cash flow minus capex is not a meaningful measure of distributable earnings for a lending institution, so net income is used instead, which means results here are more sensitive to credit-cost and provisioning assumptions embedded in reported earnings than a typical DCF on this site. Bank analysts more conventionally value names like this on price-to-book, price-to-tangible-book (SFBS trades around 2.4x book, book value per share roughly $18.10 and tangible book value per share roughly $17.97 on a post-split basis), and ROE-based frameworks rather than a cash-flow DCF; this analysis is offered as a rough cross-check against those methods, not a replacement for them. Holding-company debt of $34.75M is reported standalone rather than netted against consolidated cash, which sits at the bank subsidiary and isn't freely available to the parent. DCF results are also extremely sensitive to the terminal growth rate and WACC chosen. These are mechanical estimates from reported data, not investment advice.
