Common valuation techniques

  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): projects future free cash flows and discounts them to present value using a required rate of return (WACC). Most theoretically rigorous, but highly sensitive to growth and discount rate assumptions.
  • Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Comparables: compares a company's P/E to peers or its own history. Quick and intuitive, but earnings can be manipulated and it ignores growth differences. See P/E Ratios.
  • PEG Ratio: P/E divided by earnings growth rate, adjusting for growth. A PEG under 1 is often considered undervalued, though it's still a rough heuristic. See PEG Bands.
  • Dividend Discount Model (DDM): values a stock as the present value of future dividends, P = D / (r − g). Only works for stable, dividend-paying companies.
  • Price-to-Book (P/B): compares market price to book value. Useful for banks and asset-heavy companies; less meaningful for tech or service firms.
  • EV/EBITDA: Enterprise Value divided by EBITDA. Better than P/E for comparing companies with different capital structures or tax situations.
  • Residual Income Model: values a stock based on earnings above the cost of equity (economic profit). Works even for non-dividend payers.
  • Asset-Based Valuation: sum of all assets minus liabilities at fair market value. Most relevant for holding companies, real estate firms, or companies near liquidation.
  • Reverse DCF: starts with the current stock price and solves for the implied growth rate, then judges whether that growth is realistic.

In practice, analysts rarely rely on a single method. DCF is usually the primary valuation, sanity-checked against comparable multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA). The result is a valuation range, not a single number.
